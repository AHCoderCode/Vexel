How to check an AI answer

AI tools can explain ideas and help organise research, but a fluent answer is not proof. Verification means checking whether the important claims are supported by reliable evidence that actually says what the answer claims.

A practical guide · About 7 minutes

AI-generated responses can combine accurate facts with an incorrect date, a missing condition, a misread source, or an unsupported conclusion. The risk is easy to miss because the explanation may still sound polished. A repeatable checking routine is more reliable than judging by confidence or writing style.

First decide how much checking the claim needs

Not every sentence needs a research project. A simple brainstorming suggestion may only need your judgment. A claim that affects money, health, safety, legal obligations, a school assignment, or a current event deserves stronger evidence. Check recent information especially carefully because models may not know about changes that happened after their training or retrieval process.

Ask yourself: What would happen if this claim were wrong? The larger the consequence, the more important independent verification becomes.

Follow the evidence, not just the citation

  1. Open the source. A URL in a response does not prove the page exists, supports the claim, or was interpreted correctly.
  2. Find the exact passage or data. Search within the page for the key phrase, read the surrounding text, and note whether it is a direct statement, a prediction, an opinion, or a summary of someone else.
  3. Check the publisher. Official documentation, primary records, original research, and responsible institutions are often better starting points than an unsourced repost.
  4. Check date and scope. Confirm the publication or update date, the location, the version, and any conditions. A correct statement about one country or software version can be wrong for another.
  5. Compare independently. For a consequential claim, seek a second credible source that does not simply repeat the first source.

Example: a software feature

Suppose an assistant says a button exists in a particular version of a developer tool. Check the official documentation for that version, not a random tutorial for a newer release. If the feature moved or the version changed, the original answer may be outdated even if it was once correct.

Separate facts, interpretation, and recommendations

Break a long answer into claims that can be checked individually. “The law took effect in June” is a factual claim. “This means the policy will work well” is an interpretation. “You should choose option A” is a recommendation that may depend on your circumstances.

For each important claim, ask:

  • What evidence would make this claim true or false?
  • Does the source directly support this exact wording?
  • Is a relevant exception or competing explanation missing?
  • Is the assistant mixing a measured result with a prediction or personal judgment?

Check numbers and calculations independently

Do not assume a numeric answer is correct just because the arithmetic is formatted neatly. Recalculate important totals using a calculator or spreadsheet, inspect units, and verify the starting values. For percentages, distinguish percentage points from percent change. For dates, check the timezone and whether the interval counts calendar days or full 24-hour periods.

When a response compares options, make sure the numbers use the same definition and time period. A cost per month should not be compared directly with a cost per year without conversion.

Useful follow-up questions for an AI assistant

These questions can expose gaps, but they do not replace external verification:

  • “List the factual claims in your answer that are most important to verify.”
  • “Which parts are confirmed by the sources you cited, and which are your inference?”
  • “What assumptions are you making? What information could change the conclusion?”
  • “Could this information have changed recently? Give me an official source to check.”
  • “If you cannot verify a point, label it as uncertain rather than filling in a guess.”

Remember

A source can itself be mistaken or misleading, and two sites can copy the same error. Prefer primary evidence where possible, check context, and be willing to leave a question unresolved when evidence is insufficient.

A quick verification checklist

  • Have I identified the specific claim that matters?
  • Does the original source actually support it?
  • Are the date, location, version, and units correct?
  • Have I checked important claims against an independent source?
  • Have I distinguished evidence from inference or advice?
  • Do I understand the limits of what has been verified?

Verification is not about distrusting every answer. It is about matching the strength of evidence to the importance of the decision. AI can help you discover questions to investigate; you remain responsible for checking the answers that matter.
